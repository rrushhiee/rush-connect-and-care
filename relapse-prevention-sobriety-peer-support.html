Relapse Prevention and Sobriety Support: Practical Peer Support for Everyday Life

Published 1 September 2026 · By Rupert Rush

Relapse prevention is not just about saying no once. For many people, remaining abstinent or working towards sobriety is a process of understanding patterns, managing risk, building structure, and having the right kind of support around them before things become too heavy.

For NDIS participants, families, support coordinators and referrers, this can matter when alcohol or drug use is connected with mental health issues, isolation, boredom, drug-induced psychosis, unstable routines, or people and places that make relapse more likely.

The process

Relapse prevention starts with knowing the pattern

One of the most useful parts of relapse prevention is getting honest about triggers. Not in a harsh way, but in a practical way. A trigger might be a friend who is still using, someone offering drugs, a time of day when cravings usually show up, boredom, loneliness, payday, stress, conflict, lack of sleep, certain music, certain locations, or the feeling of having nothing meaningful planned.

Sometimes the trigger is obvious. Sometimes it is sneaky. A person might not feel at risk until they are tired, unstructured, scrolling online, alone at night, or around people who make old habits feel normal again. That is why a relapse prevention plan works best when it is simple, visible, and easy to use in the moment.

I have found it helpful to keep a copy of a relapse prevention plan in iPhone Notes. It does not need to be a perfect document. It just needs to be available when the brain starts bargaining, minimising, or trying to turn a risky idea into a reasonable one.

Practical tools

Strategies that can support abstinence and sobriety goals

Traffic light plan

Green means things are steady. Orange means warning signs are showing. Red means support needs to increase now, before the situation becomes harder to pull back from.

Delay, distract, decide

Delay the urge, distract with a planned activity, then decide again once the body and mind have had time to settle.

Change the environment

Move rooms, go outside, call someone, leave the risky setting, or get around people who support the version of you trying to stay well.

Urge surfing

Notice the craving like a wave. It can rise, feel intense, and then pass. The goal is not to love the feeling. The goal is to not act while it is strongest.

If-then planning

Make decisions before the risk arrives. If a certain friend calls, then I will not meet alone. If I feel bored at night, then I will walk, shower, call, read, or go to the gym.

Content and community

Surrounding yourself with sober content, recovery stories, peer groups, podcasts, forums, or practical reminders can help sobriety stay visible during the week.

Structure

A week with direction can reduce risk

Boredom can be underestimated. For some people, having too much empty time creates room for cravings, overthinking, old contacts, or the feeling that nothing really matters. A structured week can help protect sobriety because it gives the person something to move towards.

That structure does not need to be intense. It might include the gym, walking, socialising with safer people, reading, cooking, appointments, sport, hobbies, volunteering, a peer-support group, community access, or regular check-ins with someone steady. The point is to build a week that has enough rhythm to reduce drift.

Online communities can also help some people feel less alone. One website I found useful after a recent lapse was Talking Sober. It is not a replacement for professional support, but for some people, reading honest recovery conversations can make the next right step feel more possible.

Where RCC fits

Lower-barrier peer support alongside everyday life

RCC provides a lower-barrier, non-clinical layer of peer support that can sit alongside everyday life and, where appropriate, alongside clinical AOD services. Victorian guidance recognises peer support as people with personal experience providing mutual support and information, while alcohol and other drug counselling is delivered as a treatment service by relevant professionals.

At Rush Connect & Care, relapse prevention, abstinence and sobriety may be one example of a client goal. Support can involve connecting with the person, understanding what is happening in their week, discussing drug-related patterns, identifying triggers, reviewing what is working and what is not, and building practical routines around health, stability and community.

The tone is intentionally casual, steady and one-to-one. A client should not feel like they have to perform recovery perfectly before they can be supported. The work is about trust, openness at the client’s pace, and practical next steps that fit real life.

Escalation

When more support may be needed

Sometimes practical peer support is not enough on its own. If a person is repeatedly relapsing, feeling unsafe, experiencing severe mental health symptoms, withdrawing from substances, losing control of daily life, or thinking they may need rehab, it may be time to bring in clinical AOD services, a GP, psychiatrist, counsellor, rehab pathway, crisis service, or other professionals.

At RCC, that conversation is handled with respect. Rehab and treatment decisions belong to the client and their clinical supports. My approach is that a person has to be ready, or at least moving towards readiness, for that kind of help to work properly. The role of support can be to notice patterns, talk honestly, reduce shame, encourage safer choices, and help the person connect with the right services when they choose to.
