Why Some NDIS Participants Prefer Independent Support Workers Over Large Providers

Published 12 May 2026

Choosing the right support can make a significant difference to a participant’s confidence, comfort and overall experience with the NDIS.

While larger providers work well for many people, others prefer a smaller and more personalised approach to support. Every participant has different needs, personalities and goals, which means there is no single best style of provider for everyone.

For some participants, families and carers, working with an independent support worker or smaller NDIS provider can feel more consistent, flexible and personal.

This update is written for NDIS participants, families, carers, support coordinators and plan managers who are comparing larger providers with smaller, more personalised NDIS support options. It may also help people searching for peer support NDIS options or a peer support worker where relationship fit matters.

Provider choice

Large providers still play an important role

Large NDIS providers can offer broad service coverage, structured systems and access to larger teams. For participants with complex scheduling needs or those requiring multiple services under one organisation, this can be a strong option.

However, some participants are looking for a different type of support relationship, one that feels more individual, familiar and community-based.

Independent support

Why some participants prefer independent support

One of the biggest reasons participants move towards smaller providers or independent support workers is consistency.

Building trust takes time. Many participants feel more comfortable when they can work regularly with the same person rather than rotating staff or changing schedules. Familiarity can help create a more stable and relaxed environment, especially for participants who value routine, communication and long-term rapport.

More flexibility

Smaller services may be able to shape support around real routines, interests and practical day-to-day needs.

Direct communication

Participants, families and referrers can often communicate more directly with the person providing or coordinating support.

Stronger relationship building

Consistent one-to-one support can make it easier to build trust, understand goals and notice what helps over time.

A calmer support experience

Some participants prefer support that feels less system-focused and more familiar, grounded and community-based.

Personalised understanding

The right support worker can learn a participant's communication style, interests, routines and goals.

Support that feels respectful

For many people, good support is not only about tasks. It is about feeling understood, respected and comfortable.

Personalised NDIS support

Personalised support looks different for everyone

NDIS support work can involve many different areas of day-to-day life depending on the participant’s goals and circumstances.

Community access

Support to get out, attend places that matter, and stay connected with community life.

Transport support

Practical support around appointments, social connection, errands or community-based activities.

Routine assistance

Help building structure, motivation and steady habits that make daily life feel more manageable.

Social support

Support that makes connection, conversation and participation feel easier to approach.

Confidence building

Small, practical steps that support independence, confidence and capacity over time.

Maintaining independence

Support that adapts to the participant rather than expecting every person to fit into the same structure.

Youth mentoring and NDIS support work are different services

At Rush Connect & Care, youth mentoring and general NDIS support work are offered as separate services.

NDIS support work is available for participants across a wide range of ages and backgrounds, from younger participants through to older adults, depending on individual needs and goals.

Youth mentoring is a more specific service designed for younger people who may benefit from additional peer-style guidance, social encouragement, confidence building and community connection.

For some young people, having support that feels relatable, grounded and less clinical can help create stronger engagement and trust over time.

Peer support remains available where it is the right fit for the participant.

Right fit

The importance of finding the right fit

One of the most important parts of disability support is finding the right match between participant and support worker.

Communication

Good support depends on clear communication between the participant, worker, family and referrers where appropriate.

Consistency

Regular, familiar support can help participants feel more settled and understood.

Patience

Progress is not always instant. The right support relationship gives people room to build trust over time.

Trust

Trust is often what allows practical support, social connection and confidence building to work properly.

Understanding

Genuine understanding helps support feel less rushed, less transactional and more human.

Choice

Having different provider styles available matters because what works well for one participant may not work for another.
