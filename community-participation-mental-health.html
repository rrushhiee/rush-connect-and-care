Community participation and mental health

Community Participation: Why It Matters for Mental Health

Published 23 June 2026 · By Rupert Rush

Mental health is not only shaped by what happens in a clinic. It is also shaped by everyday life: whether we feel connected, whether we have places to go, people to see, roles to play and things that give us a sense of purpose.

If you have been wondering, “Why do I feel low?” or looking for ways to occupy your time that may help your mental health, a small, meaningful connection can be a realistic place to start. Community participation is not a luxury. It can be part of building routine, confidence and a life that feels more like your own.

Connection supports mental health

Research is clear that social connection plays a major role in mental health. The World Health Organization describes social connection as the way we relate to and interact with others, including the structure, function and quality of our relationships.

A major 2022 review found strong evidence that loneliness and social isolation are linked with poorer mental-health outcomes, especially depression and anxiety. Australian and international research also shows that belonging to social groups can protect against depression and support recovery.

Why participation helps

Community participation can support mental health in several ways. It can increase social support, reduce isolation, bring structure and routine, create enjoyable experiences, and build confidence.

It can also strengthen a person’s identity: not just as someone managing challenges, but as a teammate, volunteer, artist, walker, class member, neighbour or friend. NDIA research found that community and social activities can increase belonging, connection and inclusion, as well as confidence and safety.

It is normal for it to take time

One of the biggest myths is that joining socially should feel easy straight away. Often it does not. The first step is usually not belonging: it is simply showing up.

Many people need a few visits to feel familiar, a few more to feel comfortable, and longer still to feel that they truly belong. That is normal. The evidence supports gradual, repeated participation rather than pressure for instant transformation.

Rest versus push

A helpful way to think about participation is green, amber and red. On green days, you might feel nervous but still able to go. On amber days, it may help to reduce the dose: do a shorter outing, choose a quieter place, or go with extra support. On red days, rest and reset may be the right choice.

Pacing is not giving up. It is often what makes participation sustainable, especially when sleep, stress, transport, confidence or health are already under pressure. NDIA research notes that things such as sleep, mental and physical health, confidence and transport can affect a person’s capacity to participate.

What community access can look like

Community participation does not have to mean large events or busy groups. It can be walking with someone at a local park, going to the gym, trying a community class, joining a library or hobby group, attending a sport session, visiting a museum, going to the movies, meeting for coffee, or exploring volunteering.

NDIS guidance recognises many of these as social and recreation activities. Where extra disability-related help is needed to participate, support may be available depending on the participant’s individual goals, needs and funding.

How a little support can make a big difference

Sometimes the hardest part is not the activity itself. It is getting started. A little support can make a big difference: help with planning, transport, confidence, introductions, pacing, and building routine.

At Rush Connect & Care, community access can be about finding the right fit, taking small steps, and building confidence over time. The goal is not to rush people. It is to help people connect in ways that feel meaningful, sustainable and supportive of their wellbeing.
